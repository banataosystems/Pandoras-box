# Pandora Go-To-Market & Customer Success

Selling and delivering are the same motion at this stage. What you promise in the sale is what the delivery must prove.

## Never over-commit

Do not promise a capability that is not `production_verified`, and do not describe a roadmap item as though it exists. The proof ladder is not just an engineering convention — it is what makes a sales claim honest.

When asked whether the product does something, answer from its actual proof state. "That works today" · "That is built but not yet live" · "That is not built; here is what we could do instead" are all sellable answers. A false yes creates a delivery obligation that costs far more than the deal.

Never fabricate customers, quotes, case studies, logos, or references. A fabricated reference is discovered, and it ends the relationship it was meant to win.

## Qualification

Qualify out early and honestly. Time spent on a prospect who cannot buy is the most expensive thing in founder-led sales, because it is the scarcest resource being spent.

Establish: is this problem one they are actively trying to solve · is there a budget or a path to one · who decides and who can veto · what is the timeline and what forces it · what happens if they do nothing.

That last question is the strongest qualifier. If nothing happens when they do nothing, there is no deal — only a pleasant conversation.

## Founder-led discovery

Ask about their current process in detail. Where does it break, what does that cost, what have they already tried. Let them describe the problem before you describe the product; a pitch delivered before you understand the problem is a guess.

Talk about outcomes, not architecture. The customer does not care about migrations, proof gates, or providers — they care whether bookings stop getting lost.

## Proposals

State the outcome, the scope, the price, the timeline, and what you need from them. Be explicit about what is **not** included — undefined scope is where delivery relationships fail.

Where genuine uncertainty exists, price a paid discovery phase rather than guessing at a fixed price. Guessing produces either a loss or a renegotiation, and both damage trust.

## Pilots

Design per `pandora-commercial-validation`: real price, defined outcome, agreed success criteria, explicit continuation path. Set the success criteria **before** the pilot starts — criteria defined afterward get argued about, and the arguing is what loses the account.

## Onboarding

Onboarding is where churn is decided. Get the customer to a real outcome fast — the first genuine result matters more than a complete feature tour.

Ask for the minimum needed to start, and defer the rest. Every field on a setup form is a chance to abandon. Know the moment a customer has clearly received value ("their first booking came through the new system") and measure how long it takes to get there.

## Customer success

Watch for real churn signals: declining usage, a lapsed champion, an unresolved support issue, missed value milestones. Intervene early with something specific rather than a check-in.

Renewal is earned across the term, not negotiated at the end. A renewal conversation that begins at renewal time is already in trouble.

Expansion should follow demonstrated value, not a quota. Expanding a customer who has not yet succeeded accelerates churn.

## Churn recovery

Find out what actually happened — usually it is unrealized value, a lost champion, a real product gap, or a cost that was never justified. Ask directly; departing customers are unusually honest.

Fix what is fixable and say what is not. A churned customer who left on honest terms sometimes returns; one who left feeling misled does not.

## Case studies

Only from real customers, with permission, with real numbers where they will allow it. Describe what actually changed for them. If you cannot get permission, do not anonymize a real customer into a fictional one that reads as a different account.

## Enterprise

Enterprise capability is expensive: SSO, SCIM, audit logs, data-residency controls, private environments, security questionnaires, procurement cycles, SLAs.

**Only build it when demand and economics justify it.** A single prospect asking for SSO is not a mandate; several qualified prospects blocked on it, with contract value covering the build, is. Until then, say honestly what exists.

Security questionnaires are answered truthfully from actual state. Never claim a control you do not have — these answers become contractual, and Pandora explicitly prohibits fabricating compliance status.
